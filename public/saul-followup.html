This is the rest of the game.

Everything here is for after you hang up: what to send and when, how each objection forks depending on how John responds, the objections he has not raised yet but is holding, and the loop-close checklist. Nothing on this page states a Starpilot fact you did not hear from them.

The clock after the call

Speed is a signal to a founder who posts against bureaucracy. Slow follow-up reads as a slow operator.

  • within 2 hWrite down every number he said: hours, cost per hour, countries, rejection rate, headcount. That list is your only source for Starpilot facts from now on.
  • within 24 hSend the note. One thing he said, one thing you would do first, one number of yours. Under 150 words.
  • within 72 hIf he named a real problem, send one page: the day-30 plan for that problem with a dashboard sketch. Unasked, short, specific. This is the founder-hungry proof the posting asks for.
  • day 5No reply: one line, no pressure, one new fact of yours. Then stop. Founders answer when they answer.
  • any timeReferences: line up two people who saw you build, not just run. One who reports to you, one you reported to.

Notes to send

Three registers. Pick the one that matches how the call actually went. Fill the slots with what he really said.

strong call · he leaned in

John, thanks for the time. The number that stuck with me was [his number: cost per usable hour, rejection rate, or hours per month]. If I were starting Monday, the first thing I would build is [one instrument: the per-site turnaround dashboard, the rejection-by-cause view, the launch checklist], because it is the lever behind that number.

On the part I have not done, [customs / entity setup], here is how I would close it in the first 30 days: [one sentence, one date].

My own number from Sama, for the record: [one real number]. Happy to go deeper on any of it. Saul

neutral call · hard to read

John, thanks for walking me through the machine. Two things I took away: [fact one] and [fact two]. The question I keep coming back to is [one operational question from the call]. If useful, I can send a one-page view on how I would approach it. Either way, appreciated the directness. Saul

recover · a moment went badly

John, thanks for the conversation. One answer I gave deserved a sharper version: when you asked about [the moment], the honest answer is [the true, short answer]. I would rather you have the clear version than the fast one. The rest of what I said stands. Saul

Forked battle cards

Each objection has a branch for how he plays it: he raises it, you pre-empt it, he pushes back on your bridge, and he pushes again. The third push means concede the point and move to what you can prove.

You have never launched a country from zero.

he raises it“Correct. Sama's countries existed when I got there. I built the operation inside them and I built Change Machine's operation from nothing. Entity setup is counsel plus a checklist. First hires and first sites are the part I have done.”
you pre-emptSay it in the fit section before he asks. A named gap is a strength; a discovered gap is a liability.
he pushes back“Checklist is easy to say.” Answer: “Then let me earn it on Turkey. I go on the ground for the opening weeks, and you judge the first 10,000 hours, not the resume.”
he pushes againConcede: “If from-zero entity work is the deciding criterion, I am not the strongest candidate on that line. On every other line in the posting I am. Your call which one you are hiring for.”

You have never shipped hardware across borders.

he raises it“I have not. Week one I hire a logistics lead and set one tracked flow: ship, repair, return. Day 30 you get turnaround per site.”
you pre-emptAsk first: “How are cameras moving today, and where do they get stuck?” His answer tells you whether this is a real gap or a solved problem.
he pushes back“We cannot afford a logistics hire.” Answer: “Then it is me and a freight forwarder for the first country, and I document it into the playbook so the second country does not need me.”
he pushes againConcede and reframe: “Agreed it is a gap. It is the most learnable gap on the list, and it is not the one that loses hours. Rejected hours and slow sites lose hours. That part I know cold.”

Ten years at one company. Are you hungry?

he raises it“Seven roles, two VP seats, three functions. I stayed because the scope kept doubling. I am leaving because it stopped.”
you pre-emptDo not pre-empt this one. Pre-empting a tenure question sounds defensive. Let him ask.
he pushes back“Big-company VPs do not sit in a field in Vietnam.” Answer: “I was the escalation point for 25 engagements across four continents. Field is where the escalations were.” Then one concrete field story.
he pushes againConcede the frame, keep the fact: “You will only know by watching. Put me on the Turkey launch and decide from that.”

We need cheap, fast, and precise, with nine people.

he raises it“Retention is the unit-cost lever. Local workforce, local pay, and people who stay produce cheap hours that stay high quality. That is the Sama model and it is your model.”
you pre-emptAsk his cost per usable hour and his rejection rate before you say anything about cost. Then talk about the gap between the two.
he pushes back“Sama had hundreds of people and margin to spare.” Answer: “Sama had SLAs with a quarter of the Fortune 50 and profitability targets. Nobody spared margin. I can show you the reporting we ran it on.”
he pushes againStop defending Sama. “Fair. Tell me your number and I will tell you what I would cut first.” Then listen.

Can you actually work with our engineers?

he raises it“I ran product for the platform your kind of engineers built. My SQL is [true level]. I will own my dashboards by day 30 without pulling an engineer off capture.”
you pre-emptBring one dashboard you built and the decision it changed. Show, do not claim.
he pushes backHe asks a technical question directly. Answer at your true level, then: “Here is how I would get the rest.” Never bluff a CTO.
he pushes againConcede: “If you need an engineer in this seat, I am not one. If you need an operator engineers trust because he reads their numbers and does not waste their time, that is the job I have done for ten years.”

Precision is the moat and human review is the bottleneck.

he raises it“Keep precision, remove the bottleneck: calibration per site, tracker health before capture, auto-reject rules, sampled audit instead of full review.”
you pre-emptAsk the retargeting acceptance rate and hours delivered versus captured. If he does not have the number, that is your first dashboard.
he pushes back“Sampling will miss errors.” Answer: “Sampling with rejection-by-cause tracking finds the site or the device that produces them. Full review finds the same errors slower and hides the cause.”
he pushes againDefer to his technical lead: “Hisham owns the precision bar. I own getting there at scale. I will not set the bar; I will hit it cheaper.”

Objections he has not said yet

Anticipatory. He may be holding any of these. Have the answer ready and do not volunteer it unless the door opens.

  • Comp. The band tops at $250k base. If your Sama number was higher, decide now whether equity and ownership make up the gap. Never negotiate in the first call; do say “the range works as a starting point” only if it does.
  • Travel and Brooklyn. Weeks at a time in three regions, in person in Brooklyn. A conditional answer reads as a no. Decide before the call and say it plainly.
  • Overqualified for the seat, underqualified for the hardware. Both at once. Answer: “The seat is the whole machine. I have run the whole machine. The hardware is the newest part and the most learnable.”
  • Why not hire a founder. The posting says ex-founders will feel at home. Answer: “A founder starts things. You need someone who starts five and hands each one off. Handing off is the hard part, and it is what I have done seven times.”
  • Sama references. He may know people there. Assume he will call. Line up your own references first.
  • Why did you leave Sama in July. One sentence, forward-looking, the same sentence every time.
  • Age and energy, unspoken. Nobody will say it. Answer it with tempo: fast follow-up, short answers, a plan with dates.
  • Start date and notice. Have a date. A founder hears a vague start date as a vague yes.

Close the loop

The posting asks for three things. Make sure each has left your hands before the process moves.

  • The largest physical operation you launched or scaled, with a scale number.
  • The hardest problem, in one paragraph, with what changed.
  • The number that proves you fixed it. Before and after.
  • Your gaps, named by you first, each with a day-30 deliverable.
  • One dashboard you built, shown, with the decision it changed.
  • The operating rhythm proposal: what you decide, what he decides, how often.
  • A plain answer on travel and in-person.
  • Ask for the next step by name: “What is the next conversation, and with whom?”
