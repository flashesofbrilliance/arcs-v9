01 · Under the hood

Under the hood

What this page is

Before and after every conversation that matters, I keep a working file. This is the one I kept for last week's call, with one change: my notes about people are removed. What is left is the method: what I predicted, how I checked my own reads against the transcript, and what I changed my mind about.

It is the same discipline I would bring to Contract OS. Write the prediction down before the event, check it afterward against something outside my own head, and keep the corrections visible instead of smoothing them over.

Sections 2 to 5 are the method, one step each, in order. The last three sections are the brief I sent last week.

02 · Predict

Predict

The prediction, filed before the call

The question
One sharpened question the conversation should help answer.
What I expect to see
A specific, dated outcome, written down before the call so it can be scored after.
Planned move
What I intend to lead with, decided in advance rather than in the moment.
Belief most likely to change
The one I hold least firmly, named so I notice when it moves.
What would prove me wrong
A falsifier with a date. If it happens, the prediction failed, and I record that.

Why bother

Without a written prediction, every outcome looks like what I expected. With one, I find out how often I am right, and the misses teach me more than the hits. It is the same rule I use for a release: the pass rules are frozen before the benchmark runs.

03 · Check

Check

Checking my own reads

After the call I wrote down my reads of how it went. Then an independent reviewer, an agent that pulled the transcript itself rather than my notes, ruled on each one. Grounded means the transcript forces it. Inference means it is my read and nothing forces it. Struck means I overreached, and the read is corrected.

A sample of the rulings

04 · Update

Update

What I changed my mind about

My own beliefs only. Each moved because of something said on the call.

  • The seat. I came in framing the role as part product owner, part forward-deployed engineer. The legal engineers already do the second job, so I now read the seat as product ownership plus triage of the feature requests the enablement lawyers bring back.
  • Revenue. I expected to be pressed for revenue proof early. I was not, and the numbers I offered were not revenue numbers anyway. It is still the gap I would name first, so it stays on my list.
  • My own read of the call. I was more confident in my post-call notes than the transcript allowed. I now treat my first read of any conversation as a draft.

Why the corrections matter

The corrections are the most useful output. Pitching myself as part forward-deployed engineer would have missed, because that work already has an owner. An error like that is cheap to fix before the next conversation and expensive after it.

06 · The answer

The answer

What AI product have I owned, and how did I plan the roadmap?

The AI product I have owned end to end is ARCS, a decision tool that labels every claim confirmed or unconfirmed. A user brings a consequential question. An LLM pipeline returns a recommendation with its reasoning, what is confirmed, what is not, and a confidence state.

I built it with Claude Code agents as my engineering team. Agents do what the spec says, not what I meant, so I write the acceptance test first. What I have not had is a human engineer pushing back on a decision; I expect Dioptra's team to.

What ARCS does not prove yet is the revenue side. With Contract OS I would tie every roadmap bet to a deal it wins or an expansion it unlocks, and learn which bets travel through Icertis's customers and which through Dioptra's own deals.

Four decisions that shaped the roadmap

Each with the reason it exists.

  • Code, not the model, sets the confidence state. Code maps the model's reported signals plus the user's stated stakes to a state. The model still supplies most of those inputs; checking them against something outside the model is the next step.
  • Trust is in the data model. Each answer stores its confirmed and unconfirmed claims and its computed state, so a user can reopen it later and see why. A claim nothing can verify stays unconfirmed.
  • Success is defined before shipping. Pass rules are frozen before a benchmark runs. Two of my own predictions were wrong, and the benchmark caught both.
  • Saying no is on the roadmap. The backlog records what is deliberately not built and why.
07 · How it works

How it works

How it is enforced, with real numbers

Release gate
One pre-registered benchmark, run once, thresholds frozen: 10 checks, 8 passed, 2 failed (an edited-text index at 85.0% against an 85% kill line, and a prefilter). Neither failure was adopted. The one new feature, a lineage index, shipped only after clearing its bar: a 69.9% wall-time cut.
Safety
0 confident-wrong answers in 800 test queries.
Audit trail
Merges, deploys and publishes are logged to a ledger that refuses edits and deletes. A correction is a new row pointing at the one it replaces.
Model routing
Which model handles which job lives in one config file; a pre-commit check blocks hardcoded model IDs.
Latency and context
Not yet measured end to end in ARCS. The one timing I have is a lookup cut from 5.15 s to 1.55 s. This is what I would learn from your team first.
Full results
Pre-registration and every result, failures included, are available on request.
