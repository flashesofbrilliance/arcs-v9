The exoskeleton, not the oracle

Tribes, Vibes, Lies

I · The two decisions I closed are the least interesting ones I made

I built a system to make my decisions learnable. It writes each one down before I act: the question, who else is in the room, the time window, how sure I already am. Then it waits for me to come back and say what happened. After 247 of those receipts, I had closed the loop on exactly two.

I am opening with that number because most people in my position would hide it, and because it is the clearest picture I have of what is broken in human judgment. We decide constantly. We almost never go back and check. Not because we are lazy, but because the moment of deciding and the moment of knowing are months apart, and nothing in our lives connects them.

But the number is also misleading, and the way it misleads is the point of this essay. The decisions that saved me the most time over the past year do not appear in it at all. They were the ones where I said no. I passed on a client, a prospect, a call. Nothing happened next. There was no project to go right or wrong, so there was never an outcome to log. A no closes against a counterfactual, and nobody can observe a counterfactual.

So the honest version of my confession is this: the two loops I closed are the least interesting ones. The most valuable judgment I exercised is structurally invisible to any ledger of outcomes, including the one I built. That is not a bug in my system. It is the hardest problem in learning from your own decisions, and I had to run into it 247 times before I could see it.

II · Answers got cheap. Judgment that compounds did not.

In the past three years the price of a competent answer to almost any question has fallen toward zero. Ask a model how to structure a deal, read a contract, size a market, draft the hard email. You will get something good in seconds. The people who were paid for having answers are finding out that answers were never the scarce part.

What stays scarce is judgment, and specifically judgment that gets better over time. Not the ability to decide once. The ability to decide, find out, and decide better the next time. That loop is the only thing that separates a person with twenty years of experience from a person with one year of experience repeated twenty times.

Almost nobody runs that loop deliberately. We run it by accident, through memory, and memory is a flattering narrator. It remembers the calls we got right, forgets the ones we got wrong, and quietly rewrites what we believed at the time so that it matches what happened. Ask anyone whether they saw a bad hire coming and they will say yes. Ask them to show you where they wrote that down beforehand and the room goes quiet.

So here is the market as I see it. Every tool being built right now sells cheaper answers. Nobody is selling the loop. Nobody is selling a way to find out, in your own record, whether your judgment is actually improving or whether you have just gotten more confident.

Nobody is selling the loop.
III · Prosthetic or exoskeleton

There are two ways to build a tool for a human capacity, and they point in opposite directions.

A prosthetic replaces the function. You lose a leg, the prosthetic walks for you. It is a gift when the capacity is gone. It is a quiet disaster when the capacity was merely tired. Hand a working muscle a prosthetic and the muscle does what unused muscles do. It fades.

An exoskeleton does something else. The muscle still fires. You still do the lifting. The frame adds strength, holds you steady under load, and, if it is well built, shows you your own form while you move. Take it off and you are not weaker than before. Over time you are stronger, because you have been lifting the whole time.

Almost every AI tool on the market today is a prosthetic for judgment. It thinks so that you do not have to. It is excellent at that, which is exactly the problem. The capacity it replaces is the one capacity that was going to compound, and a few years from now a lot of capable people are going to notice that they can no longer decide anything without asking first.

I am describing a different kind of instrument. One that never tells you what to decide. It makes you write down what you believe before the pressure peaks, holds that belief steady while the room pushes on it, and then shows you, in your own record, whether you were right. An exoskeleton for the part of you that decides.

The oracle gives you an answer. The exoskeleton gives you a receipt.

IV · The part of you that decides has no receipts

The front of your brain, behind your forehead, does a handful of jobs that no other part does. It holds a problem in mind. It stops the first impulse long enough to consider a second one. It estimates how sure you really are. It notices when the world did not match the plan. And it replays past episodes so you can learn from them. Those five jobs are what we mean when we say someone has good judgment.

They are also the five jobs that fail first under pressure, fatigue, and a persuasive person across the table. The impulse wins. The confidence inflates. The mismatch gets explained away. The replay never happens because the next decision is already here.

Every one of those jobs can be done outside your head, on paper, where pressure cannot reach it. That is the whole design. One instrument per job:

Five jobs, five instruments

Holding the problemA receipt: the decision written down before you act, as a fixed coordinate. Stopping the impulseA prep step that asks for the strongest argument against your own instinct, before the call. Gauging confidenceA gate that reads your conviction and returns a state: clear, contested, forked, or red. Catching the missClosing the outcome: did it land, and how much do you regret it. Replaying the episodeA record you can scrub back through, decision by decision.

A functional analogy, not neuroscience. Nothing here claims to optimize a cortex; it claims that each of these jobs can be given a frame to lean on.

I want to be precise about what that table claims. I am not going to tell you this optimizes your prefrontal cortex, because I cannot measure that and neither can anyone selling you a supplement. What I can tell you is that these five jobs are the ones your brain is worst at when it matters most, and each of them can be given a frame to lean on.

That is the breadcrumb. If an exoskeleton can hold one of these jobs steady, it can hold all five. And if it can hold all five for one person, the record it produces is something no general purpose model can ever offer, because it is made of your decisions and nobody else's.

V · Tribes, vibes, lies

The decision I make most often is whether to let someone in. A client, a prospect, a call, a collaboration. Over the past year I have made that call more than any other, and it has taught me that there are exactly three ways to read a person, and they come in a fixed order of how much you can trust them.

Tribes. Who are they with, and who do they keep around. This is the cheapest read and the one most open to bias. It is also where the biggest filter lives. At some point I stopped screening quid pro quo operators one at a time and simply unsubscribed from the whole way of relating. Anyone who keeps a running ledger of favors, and checks it early and out loud, is telling you how the relationship will end.

Vibes. The gut read in the room. This is real signal, built from more pattern matching than you could ever write down. It is also impossible to defend, to yourself or to anyone else, and it is the first thing you will override when the logo is big enough or the invoice is late enough. A vibe is a hypothesis. Most people treat it as a verdict, in both directions.

Lies. Not the dramatic kind. The small, ordinary gap between what a person says they will do and what they then do. They said they would send the deck Thursday. They said they would try the thing. They said they would make the intro. Then Thursday came. This is the only one of the three reads that produces a fact you can write down and check. It is also the one that predicts the thing that actually kills engagements, because someone who will not follow through on a small commitment before the money is on the table will not follow through on a large one after.

So the three words are a descent. From the read you cannot prove, to the read you cannot defend, to the one that leaves a receipt. The pressure of a live decision pushes you back up that ladder, toward tribe and vibe, because they are fast. The job of the exoskeleton is to carry you down it before the pressure arrives.

One clarification that a sharp reader will want. Screening for symmetric value sounds like quid pro quo. It is the opposite. Quid pro quo settles the ledger every transaction. Symmetry is measured over the whole relationship, and the people you want are the ones who never check the ledger because it has never needed checking.

VI · The swipe-left ledger

A yes costs months to evaluate. A no costs the minutes you spent deciding. Only the yes ever gets a verdict. That asymmetry is why the instrument for saying no has to be written down, because nothing downstream will ever tell you whether it worked.

Mine is three dials and one tripwire.

Three dials, one tripwire

Dial 1 · Was this a good use of my time?Asked afterward, every time. It is the loop closer. It is what trains the other two. Dial 2 · Was the value symmetric?Not did they pay, not was it interesting. Did value flow both ways, or was I the only one carrying weight. Dial 3 · Is this the right time?A good opportunity in the wrong window is still a pass. Most frameworks have no slot for this, so people take good things at bad moments and call the result bad luck. Tripwire · Said one thing, did anotherA thing they said they would do, a thing they then did, and a diff. Trip it and the conversation ends, whatever the dials say.

The dials are gradients. They produce a yellow, a lean, a maybe. The tripwire is binary. It is the gap between stated and revealed. I do not have to probe for it. The prospect runs the test on themselves. I only have to write down what they said and check.

Two cases, so this does not stay abstract.

Placeholder · the pass What they said, in their words. What they then did not do. The pull toward yes that was in the room at the time, and which dial it was pulling on. The moment the tripwire fired. Anything learned later that confirmed or contradicted the pass. Two or three paragraphs.
Placeholder · the yes One that cleared all three dials and the tripwire, and went as expected. This one matters as much as the pass, because it proves the instrument is not just a reflex for saying no. One or two paragraphs.

Here is what I cannot claim. I cannot prove the passes were right. The counterfactual is gone. What I can show is time recovered, and criteria that held under pressure when a vibe alone would have folded. That is a weaker claim than a win rate and a far more honest one, and I would rather sell you the honest one.

VII · The gut did not get better. The record did.

I believe I have gotten better at reading people who want to take more than they give. Every experienced person believes that about themselves. Almost none of them can prove it, and I have just spent several pages telling you why: the person I passed on cannot show you the damage they would have done, and the version of me from two years ago cannot show you that he would have said yes.

So I am not going to make that claim. I am going to make a smaller one that I can actually defend.

What improved is not my gut. It is that my gut now has a record. I wrote down that they said they would send it Thursday. Thursday came. The diff got logged. Over enough prospects, the tells stopped being vibes and became a pattern I could point to. A dozen phrasings that preceded a no-show. A particular kind of enthusiasm that never turned into a calendar invite. The skill did not get better in my head. It got better on paper, where I could check it.

That is the exoskeleton thesis stated from the inside. The oracle version is: trust me, I can read people now. The exoskeleton version is: I do not have to trust myself, because I kept receipts, and here is what they show.

I do not have to trust myself. I kept receipts.

And this is why a record of your own decisions compounds in a way no general tool can match. Year three is worth more than year one. Not because the software improved, but because the ledger did, and the ledger is made of your calls, under your pressure, against your criteria. Nobody else has that data. Nobody else can.

The reason I cannot prove I got better is the same reason this instrument exists. If I had been keeping the record from day one, the proof would be sitting in it.

VIII · Thirty people, thirty closed decisions

The instrument I have been describing exists. I have been running my own decisions through it for the better part of a year, and it is why I can tell you the number 247 instead of a feeling about how it has been going. What it does not have yet is enough closed loops to say anything about calibration, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

The statistics are blunt about this. Below roughly thirty closed outcomes per group of users, any claim that your judgment matches reality is noise dressed as insight. The system I built refuses to report calibration until that threshold is crossed. It literally returns the word insufficient and the count. I think that refusal is the most honest line of code I have written.

So here is what I am looking for. Thirty people who make consequential calls about who to work with, what to pursue, and when. Who are willing to write down what they believe before each one and come back to close the loop on thirty of them. In return you get the full instrument, the heavier review mode for your high-stakes calls, and something nobody has ever been able to hand you: the first honest curve of your own judgment against what actually happened.

Bring your own kill criteria. Let the frame hold them for thirty decisions. Then see how often you would have bent them.

The cohort is thirty because that is the number that makes the result true, not because scarcity sells. When it fills, it closes, and the next one opens only when the first has its curve.

Answers are cheap now. Keep receipts.
IX · Three tributaries

Every compass reading I run ends with three branches instead of a conclusion. This one should too.

The question under the question. You came here asking how to read people better. The deeper one is: what would you have to write down, today, so that in a year you could prove whether you did? If you cannot name it, you are not learning. You are accumulating confidence.

The uncomfortable one. The stated-vs-revealed test runs on you too. Go back through the last month of things you told other people you would do. Count the Thursdays that came and went. Whatever that number is, it is the number someone else has written down about you.

The adjacent one. A pilot does not trust the feeling of level flight. The instruments exist because the inner ear lies most convincingly exactly when the visibility is worst. Judgment under pressure is instrument flying. The question is not whether your gut is good. It is whether you are willing to fly by the panel when the gut and the panel disagree.
