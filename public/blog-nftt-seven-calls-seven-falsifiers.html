Notes from the Trenches // Closing the loop

Seven Calls, Seven Falsifiers

I wrote down what would prove me wrong, then went back when the answer could no longer be avoided. Some calls held. Some missed. Two of my own readings of a primary source were wrong, and one question about January still has no owner.

Draft, Oct 6, 2026 · Calls dated at publication · Secondary-source figures are labeled as secondary · Not investment advice

I · The only honest kind of prediction

Between January 24 and February 25, 2026, I published seven pieces on tokenized assets, stablecoins, custody and market structure. Each carried a falsifier, a sentence written in advance that says what would prove the call wrong. A forecast without one is a mood. This is the accounting, and I did not rewrite the falsifiers after the fact.

A word on terms, since I will use them. A stablecoin is a token pegged to a currency, here the dollar. USDC (USD Coin) is one, issued by Circle. USD1 is another, issued by World Liberty Financial. RWA (real-world asset) tokenization means putting claims on things like Treasury bills onto a blockchain. The SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) is the United States securities regulator.

II · What held, and what missed

The first piece forecast that about 62% of a roughly $35B tokenized real-world-asset market would become structurally vulnerable in the first quarter and migrate to compliant rails. Distributed RWA value on the tracker rwa.xyz read $38.8B on October 5. There was no collapse, and I record that call as missed on its headline.

The scorecard as resolved

MissedThe RWA filtering event, and the custody cascade I built on it. No custodian freeze wave, no margin wave, no collateralized-loan-obligation downgrades found. FiredThree of my own falsifiers. The market-structure bill's Section 404 yield text was softened in a May 1 compromise, and a Senate cloture vote on the bill failed 49 to 50 on September 15. USDC returned to its December level. And gold fell more than 20% from its peak: about $5,586 intraday on January 29 (futures), then about 27% lower by March 23 and about 29% lower by June 30. PartialDeposit tokens: JPM Coin is live on Base, and a bank group plans a tokenized-deposit network for the first half of 2027. Base announced on February 18 that it is leaving the OP Stack, and I leave that one open. HeldThe direction on bank-led settlement and on stablecoin rules. The timing and size of the cascades did not hold.

The falsifiers resolved cleanly. The confidence I attached to the calls did not deserve to travel with them.

One more thing on the ledger: two of the pieces carried an in-house "S-TIER" grade, and their central premise failed on a read of the primary text. I no longer cite those grades. A score you assign yourself measures your own patience.

III · Correction one: the statement that did not say it

Two pieces said the SEC staff statement of January 28 classified tokenized Treasury bills and DeFi wrappers such as wrapped staked ether (wstETH) as non-compliant. I went back and read it twice. It restates that securities law applies on-chain and sorts tokenization into three kinds: issuer-sponsored, third-party custodial and third-party synthetic. It does not name Treasury bills. It does not name wstETH. It does not name wrappers.

The tripwire hit on the date and missed on the content. A cascade built on a document that does not say it has no trigger.
IV · Correction two: the baseline I set too high

I defined USDC "recovery" as a return to about $80B. The December level was $75B to $77B: $75.3B on December 31, $75.2B by February 28, with a $79.6B peak on March 17 (DefiLlama). Against the real baseline my own falsifier had fired, and my first draft of this very post read a September snapshot and missed it. The error is a small one in size and a large one in kind, which is why I am keeping it on the page.

V · The January drop, partly attributed

USDC supply fell about $5.7B to $6.6B between January 15 and January 31, depending on the source: DefiLlama shows $76.7B to $70.1B, CoinGecko $75.7B to $70.0B. Two of my pieces called it forced institutional liquidation after that SEC statement. The statement names nothing that would force a sale, and the drop began before it. So I tested everything I could with public data, and I will tell you where it stands, not what it proves.

Two legs, two partial explanations

Jan 16 to 22About $3.1B out. This leg fits a policy and macro sequence: tariff threats over Greenland, a fight over yield rules in the crypto market-structure bill, and the Treasury Secretary attributing the selloff to a six-standard-deviation move in Japan's ten-year bond (as relayed by a news-flow account on X). It also fits traders who had borrowed to bet on rising prices being forced out: about $1.09B liquidated on January 20 (secondary reporting). Inference, not proof. Jan 22 to 31USDC fell $3.55B (DefiLlama) or $4.39B (CoinGecko). Over the same days USD1 gained $1.89B, after Binance launched a holding-rewards campaign for it on January 23 ($40M of tokens to USD1 holders on Binance, secondary reporting). That is about 43% to 53% of the leg. The leg also overlaps the Fed-chair nomination of Kevin Warsh on January 30 and about $1.71B of liquidations that day (secondary reporting). Not shownThat the USD1 mints were funded by USDC redemptions. Overlap in time is not a flow. Circle does not publish who redeems, and a coin in a cold wallet or a frozen vault stays in circulation.

I would put it as a leading suspect with its own caveat, not as a finding.

I checked and could not support a long list of other causes: regulation-forced selling, the Iran war (it began February 28), sanctions, credit stress, foreign selling of Treasuries, a flight to Solana, hacks, and a delayed October cascade. Weekly correlations with bitcoin, bill yields, spreads and the dollar never cleared a multiple-comparison bar after more than a dozen tests, so a couple of borderline hits is what chance would give.

I also left some stories out of the causal account on purpose. Stories about pardons, officials' business ties and an executive's dispute with World Liberty are alleged and unproven in what I could source, and I found no flow link between them and USDC supply. They carry no weight here.

VI · What I would trust, and what I would not

Figures from the Treasury, the SEC and company filings are primary. Several others came through secondary reporting, and I have said so where they appear, including the Tether reserve figures, the January liquidation totals and the Binance campaign details. I have not checked them against a second source. The decisive test is also still undone: it needs an entity-level list of USDC redemptions from a paid data vendor or Binance's own conversion data.

The unproven part stays marked unproven. That is the whole discipline.

The rule I am keeping for the next calls: name the falsifier first, read the primary text twice, and correct in public when a number is wrong.
