01 · The One Clear Note

Strip away every side conversation and one note keeps humming underneath all six calls:

Louisville Brands is very good at making things and unproven at deciding which things to make. The company has real hits — the #1 best-selling ready-to-drink product in its data set, bourbon releases that sell out in under a minute. It also built a pumpkin-seed product nearly 100% of the way, shipped it, and then had to rebuild it without shells because nobody pressure-tested the assumption before the money went out.

That gap — between great instincts and unexamined bets — is exactly what ARCS closes. Not by making decisions for anyone, but by making every big bet write down its assumptions, its risks, and its exit rule before a dollar moves. Test small, read the real signal, then kill it or scale it.

2 · The Pumps Lesson — install "test small" as a rule

Proof point

The pumpkin-seed line was built nearly 100% of the way — brand, identity, product — and shipped with shells on. The market said the shells made them a chore to eat. The product was rebuilt shell-less after launch. A $2–5K side-by-side test (shelled vs. unshelled) before the run would have caught it. This isn't a criticism; it's the company's own best evidence that a pre-spend checkpoint pays for itself.

NextUse Pumps as the case study inside the Justin conversation — his own data, not a vendor's slide.

05 · The Five-by-Five — The Whole Case on One Screen

Five questions, five answers each: the who-what-when-where-why, the how, the decisions on the table, the risks of doing nothing, and what a pilot actually hands over. (Five lanes, five deep — 5×5×5×5×5. Read down any column and you have the pitch.)

The 5 W's

  1. Who: Justin (CEO), Stanley (founder), Jeben (partner/creative), the fund's investor, the board (yes, including Jim).
  2. What: a decision system — one live bet per session, written receipt, exit rule agreed before spend.
  3. When: now — a funding round just closed or is closing, and the next-bag decision is live.
  4. Where: Louisville — the factory, the c-store shelf, and the Japan pipeline.
  5. Why: Pumps proved the current process learns after the money is spent.

The 5 How's

  1. How it works: 60–90 min on one decision → a one-page receipt everyone signs.
  2. How much: $5K / $10K / $15K per month by depth; 90-day pilot; first session free.
  3. How long: 90 days, with a month-one exit if the receipts aren't earning their keep.
  4. How it's measured: every receipt is scored later — expected vs. what actually happened.
  5. How it stops: pre-agreed stop-loss rules — on every bet, and on the engagement itself.

5 Decisions on the Table

  1. The next bag — mango or something the trucker actually rebuys.
  2. The $20K/mo vendor — sign, negotiate tripwires, or pass.
  3. Yokai's next drop — price, volume, licensing.
  4. Brand family — one look or many.
  5. The factory's idle 60% — fill, co-pack, or restructure.

5 Risks of Doing Nothing

  1. Another Pumps — full build, then the market grades the homework.
  2. Vendor lock-in — $240K/year committed with no exit rule.
  3. The echo chamber — a room of agreement plus an AI that flatters.
  4. The window — new capital deployed the old way.
  5. Brand sprawl — every product paying full price for every customer.

5 Things a Pilot Hands Over

  1. Decision receipts — the written record of every major call.
  2. Stop-loss rules — a tripwire on each live bet.
  3. Pre-market test results — real buyer signal before production.
  4. A board-ready record — proof of discipline for the fund.
  5. A quarterly outside read — the 15th rock the room can't see.

The terms a board would use, translated. ROI (return on investment): base case, every dollar in returns roughly $3.50 of avoided waste and captured value within the year — about a 250% return. MOIC (multiple on invested capital — "how many times over do I get my money back"): ~3.5× base, ~8× best. IRR (the annualized rate of return): honestly, IRR is built for money that comes back over years; here the money comes back in weeks, which makes the annualized figure look absurd (triple digits). The plain version is better: the fee pays for itself the first time it stops one bad production run or one bad vendor year. Payback period: roughly the first prevented misstep — call it 60–75 days in the base case.

The stop-loss framing — and it cuts both ways. Today, the cost of a bad bet is uncapped: a vendor deal quietly eats $240K, a launch strands inventory, a Japan expansion eats a year of the CEO's attention. The pilot converts that uncapped exposure into one capped, known cost ($30K), the way insurance does — a stop-loss on wasted opportunity cost. And the engagement obeys its own rule: if after month one the receipts aren't obviously worth more than they cost, stop, out $10K, keep the receipts. ARCS carries the same tripwire it installs.

Every avoided-cost figure above is an estimate for discussion, grounded in what Jeben shared, not in Louisville Brands' books. Session one replaces these with real numbers — that's part of the deliverable.

08 · The Prove-It Test — Defensibility Before a Dollar of Production

The lowest-risk way to evaluate all of this: don't buy a retainer, run one live decision through the full loop and judge the output. Thirty days, and the only new spend is the market test itself.

  • Week 1 — the session (free). Justin + Jeben + Zach, 60–90 minutes, on the next-bag decision. Output: a one-page receipt — options considered, what we're assuming, what breaks the thesis, the recommended path, and the pre-agreed kill/scale line.
  • Weeks 2–4 — the market test (~$2–5K). Three bag concepts (mango included — it competes fairly), put in front of 100–200 real convenience-store buyers: store-counter intercepts, a simple pre-order page, or both. Cheap, fast, and directionally honest — the exact test that would have caught the shells on Pumps.
  • Day 30 — the read. The data either clears the pre-agreed line or it doesn't. Scale the winner with evidence the board can see, or kill it having spent thousands instead of a production run. Either outcome proves whether the market is actually defensible before anything gets built.
What this proves beyond the product: if the receipt from this one loop isn't obviously worth more than it cost, the 90-day pilot conversation ends there — no hard feelings, and Louisville Brands keeps the receipt and the test data.

09 · Three Parallel Paths — The Same Bet, Three Ways It Travels

Every bet Louisville Brands makes walks one of three routes through the same six stages. The first two are visible from the trailhead. The third is the dangerous one, because its symptoms feel like rewards — it feels better than discipline at every step, right up until the market grades it.

The survivable path

Feels slow. Compounds.

  1. Spark: the idea is written down as a hypothesis, not a decision.
  2. Pressure test: someone is paid to attack it; the mango competes fairly.
  3. Small contact: $2–5K test in front of real buyers, kill/scale line pre-agreed.
  4. Commitment: the survivor gets sized capital with a written exit rule.
  5. The read: results scored against the receipt — expected vs. actual.
  6. Next bet: starts smarter; the record compounds.
The treacherous path

Known risks, taken knowingly.

  1. Spark: gut call plus partial data, under time pressure.
  2. Pressure test: skipped — the deadline is the argument.
  3. Small contact: skipped — "we know this customer."
  4. Commitment: full build, no exit rule; vendor signed without tripwires.
  5. The read: ambiguous results argued in meetings, not against a record.
  6. Next bet: starts with scar tissue but no lesson bank.

Sometimes survivable — but only by luck, and the luck isn't recorded either.

The magical-thinking path

Feels best. Costs most.

  1. Spark: an AI mockup in minutes — it feels finished, so it must be.
  2. Pressure test: the room agrees, the AI flatters — unanimity mistaken for evidence.
  3. Small contact: skipped — momentum mistaken for validation.
  4. Commitment: ~100% built before the first outside signal — the Pumps route.
  5. The read: surprise filed as bad luck; history quietly rewritten.
  6. Next bet: same confidence, same priors, bigger stakes.

The symptom chart

Magical thinking announces itself in phrases that feel like health. Each one is an early symptom of a specific hazard already incubating:

The phrase (symptom)What it feels likeWhat it's incubating (hazard)
"It's basically done — look at the bag."CompletionValidation skipped; the mockup becomes the security blanket.
"Everyone's on board."AlignmentPhase-lock — no falsifier left in the room; the 15th rock stays unseen.
"We've come too far to stop now."CommitmentSunk cost steering the wheel; exposure now uncapped.
"That was just bad luck / bad timing."ResilienceThe lesson escapes unrecorded; the same mistake re-arms.
"We always knew that could happen."WisdomHindsight rewrite — the record says otherwise, or would, if one existed.
Why the receipts are the antidote and not just the record: every one of these symptoms works by editing memory — what was believed, who agreed, what was promised. A decision receipt is memory that can't be edited after the fact. It doesn't argue with magical thinking in the moment; it just quietly makes the rewrite impossible later, which is what makes the next bet start from the truth.
