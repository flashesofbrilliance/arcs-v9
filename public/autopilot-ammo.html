The three-modes case

The seat needs someone who moves between three modes, not someone who picks one

For Joe, on the CTO seat at the client he's representing.
The one-line version

This isn't really an IC-versus-manager question. It's whether someone can move fluidly between writing the code, running the team day to day, and operating as a technical partner to the CEO, without friction between the three. That switching is the actual skill the seat needs, and it's a pattern already in my record, not a claim.

The three modes, each with a real example

1

Still building: I ship production code every week, solo, not for a demo.

2

Running the team: I've directed a cross-functional pod of up to twelve people, engineering, design, business, and marketing, and led global launches through real regulatory complexity (an international expansion navigated around GDPR and data-sovereignty rules, plus platform work under SEC, FINRA, and HIPAA).

3

Partnering with the top: I've delivered 40-plus product demonstrations directly to C-suite stakeholders, converting the large majority to a pilot, and worked in direct proximity to founders and executive leadership as the technical partner in the room.

The one thing worth naming directly

My default preference is Senior IC, player-coach, pacecar, not leading with management as an identity. That's a preference about how I like to work, not a gap in what I've done: I've held real manager and leadership roles, at meaningful scale, when the situation called for it. If the seat wants someone who spends most of their time on formal 1:1s and performance cycles for their own sake, say so and we can talk about whether that's the right fit. If it wants someone who moves between all three modes as the work demands, that's exactly the shape I am.

A better way to test it than a title or a tenure number

Rather than take either of our words for it, test the three modes on their own terms: the architecture and build rounds as written, a real scenario for how I'd stand up a small team to run itself well, and a conversation with the CEO about how technical decisions get made at the strategic level.

That gives you an actual signal on all three, instead of a guess on one.

None of this is a reason not to talk. It's the reason to talk about the right things. Happy to go deeper on any of it, on a call or here.
