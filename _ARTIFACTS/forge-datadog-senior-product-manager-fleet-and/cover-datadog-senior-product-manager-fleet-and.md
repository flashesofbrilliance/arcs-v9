Hi Datadog team,

I build complex, cross-functional products for technical operators who need infrastructure visibility and control at scale. That is the short version of why this role fits.

Here is the longer one.

The fleet and lifecycle management problem is, at its core, a sequencing and scope problem: how do you expand a unified control plane to cover an ever-growing set of software components, each with its own operational model, without fracturing the experience for the operator standing in front of it? I have lived that problem. At ARCS I own the full roadmap, architecture tradeoffs, and phased capability expansion across a product that serves both human operators and AI agents acting programmatically on their behalf. The MCP server and tool-calling interfaces I built were not afterthoughts; they were first-class surfaces, because I knew automated tooling would need to operate the same control plane a human uses. That is exactly the dual-audience design challenge you describe for fleet.

Two things in the JD made me want to write specifically.

First, the framing of "what does managed mean for each new component" is the right question, and it is a harder one than it looks. Consistency of experience and consistency of operational model are not the same thing, and collapsing them is how unified platforms become lowest-common-denominator platforms. At Twill Health I ran exactly this sequencing exercise across three AI-enabled product lines: drove go-forward on two, killed the third before it consumed further engineering time. The discipline is the same: define what managed means for this component, validate it with design partners, then sequence it in.

Second, the cross-team alignment piece is real work. I have driven roadmap alignment across engineering, design, and go-to-market without direct authority, at ARCS as a solo operator and in embedded engagements at Axon and Twill where I did not own the engineering org. The skill is translating ambiguity into a sequenced plan that multiple teams can build against, and then holding the narrative steady as priorities shift.

Datadog's moat in observability is the unified surface: one place, full stack, no context switching. Fleet is the extension of that promise to the software layer itself. I would want to own that.
