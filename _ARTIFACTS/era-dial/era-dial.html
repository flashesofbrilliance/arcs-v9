Concept toy, not validated

One screen, four decades

One desktop, redrawn on the display each year's software documented as its minimum. Dial the year and the same screen gets its grid, palette and chrome from that era. The panel reports a measured band limit. It shows a reading and never refuses a frame.

    On the desktop: drag the window by its title bar, click an icon to select it and open the window, click the window's close box. With the desktop focused, arrow keys move the window and Enter toggles it. The clock is the real local time.

    The band limit is where 99% of horizontal spectral energy ends, in cycles per frame width, over 16 sampled rows (the statistic in scripts/sharpness_cutoff.py). It is a reading, not a verdict. Rendered interface graphics with crisp edges are the case where this statistic is weakest (rounds 2 and 4 of the pattern note), and a later year can read lower than an earlier one. That inversion is the finding, not a bug.

    Sources and what is not claimed

    Each row is the documented minimum or baseline display mode, not the typical user's setup. The windows are generic and carry no logos or product artwork. The widgets (window, icons, close box, clock) are drawn on the canvas, so they degrade with the era; the controls above and this text stay crisp on purpose. There is no sound: music needs audio we have rights to use. Nothing here certifies that a frame looks like its year, and there is no refusal gate.

    • 1984, first Macintosh: 512 x 342, 1-bit. Sources: Wikipedia, Carleton vintage computing archive.
    • 1992, Windows 3.1: 640 x 480, 16 colours as the baseline mode. Secondary source only: OSNews. The exact VGA palette values here are the usual ones and are not sourced.
    • 1995, Windows 95: 640 x 480, 16 colours as the documented minimum. Secondary source only: PurePC (Polish). Its default mode was not found.
    • 2001, Windows XP: 800 x 600 as the documented minimum, per ITPro Today. Colour depth was not found, so this row is drawn unquantized.
