Notes from the Trenches // On hiring builders

The Lagging Indicator

A timed coding round tells you how someone used to build. For people who now build through agents, the signal worth gating on is how they check the work, and that is something you can test.

Draft · unpublished · receipts drawn from my own repository

I · A rule from the revenue side

There is a rule every operator learns the expensive way: if you treat a lagging indicator as a leading one, you steer by the wake. Closed revenue tells you what last quarter's pipeline was worth. It cannot tell you whether this quarter's pipeline is any good. The data is accurate. It just answers an older question.

I think much of technical hiring is doing this right now. A timed round where you write code by hand, from memory, under observation, measures something real. It measures practice: years of typing the loop, recalling the method, holding the stack in your head. That practice happened in the past. The round reports it faithfully, the way closed revenue does.

The question a team is actually asking has moved. It is no longer only whether this person can produce code. It is whether this person can be trusted with a system that now produces code faster than any of us can read it.

II · What the old gate gets right

Before I argue with it, the strongest case for the timed round deserves a fair hearing, because it is not a strawman.

Reading code is not optional. When an agent writes two hundred lines in a minute, someone has to know whether those lines are right. A person who cannot follow a loop, spot an off-by-one, or tell a race condition from a typo is not reviewing anything. They are approving it. Stripped to its core, the hand-coding round asks something fair: do you understand what you are looking at?

Reading and judging code is a real signal. The trouble starts when we test it the slow way and call the speed the skill.

So I am not arguing that literacy has stopped mattering. I am arguing that a stopwatch, a blank editor and no tools test the oldest, narrowest version of it, and miss most of what now separates a careful builder from a fast one.

III · The leading indicators

If the old round measures past practice, what predicts whether someone will build well with agents? From a few thousand commits of building this way, these are the behaviours I would want to watch, in roughly the order they happen.

What to watch instead

Before the promptDo they break the problem down first, and say what done looks like, before asking a model anything? Before the codeDo they write the test, or the check, that would fail if the answer were wrong? After the outputDo they catch what the model got wrong, and explain why it was wrong, not just that it was? Before the shipAre the rules in place before anything merges: gates that refuse bad changes rather than reviewers who might notice them? After the shipDo they write down what they decided and what they expected, so the next person can check them against what happened?

None of these needs a stopwatch. Each one leaves a trace you can read.

IV · Receipts from my own repository

I can only speak to how I work, so here is the shape of it, with the parts that are still unproven marked as unproven.

Merges go through a script that pins the exact commit being merged and refuses if any check is still pending or failing. It has refused me twice when a branch moved under me, which is the point: the rule was in place before I needed it, so I did not have to be careful in the moment. One honest limit: it can only wait on the checks that exist, and with my CI switched off to save money right now, that is fewer checks than it sounds.

Pre-commit gates block whole classes of mistake rather than single instances: a model name typed by hand, a file moved while something still points to the old path, a page whose text wraps badly at some screen width. When a bug comes back twice, the fix is not just the patch. It is a check that fails the build on the entire class.

The rule is that every merge, deploy or publish ends with a decision receipt: what I expected, what I confirmed, what I could not confirm. Measured, about three in four do so far. The ledger refuses edits; a correction is a new row that names the old one. What is not yet proven: whether these receipts make my calls better over time. There are not enough closed outcomes to say, so that scoring stays switched off until there are.

None of this shows up in a timed round. All of it is what I would want to see if I were hiring someone to build with agents next to me.

V · A better gate

The fix is not to drop the test of understanding. It is to test understanding the way the work actually happens, and then make the candidate defend it live.

A two-part gate

1 buildA take-home, built with the candidate's own tools: their editor, their agents, their rules. Untimed or generously timed. Ask for the tests and the decision notes along with the code. 1a the hidden edgeWrite the spec the way stakeholders really talk: finance needs the totals to reconcile, legal needs an audit trail, sales wants it to feel smart. Never say which parts must be deterministic. A strong candidate finds the edges on their own: plain code where the answer has to be the same every time, a model only where judgment helps, and a person wherever a mistake cannot be undone. 2 defendA live session on that work. Walk me through it. Change this requirement now. Where did the model mislead you, and how did you catch it? What would break first in production?

The take-home shows how they build. The defense shows they understand what they built. Faking both at once is much harder than gaming either one alone.

The best take-home I have done worked exactly this way. The brief set the boundaries through what each stakeholder needed, and never said where the deterministic edges were. Finding them was the test. That is the skill that matters when a model sits in the middle of the system: knowing where it must not be allowed to decide.

A side effect worth naming: a format like this is also kinder to people whose minds do not do their best work against a clock, and who check their work by going back over it. That is a large share of careful engineers.

VI · Steer by the bow

Closed revenue is worth tracking. So is hand-coding fluency. Both are honest reports of the past. The mistake is gating on them as if they predicted the future, when the future is someone directing a system that writes faster than they can read, and the only safe version of that person is one who has built the habit of checking.

Test the checking. It leaves receipts.
