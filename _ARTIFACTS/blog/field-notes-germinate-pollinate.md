## The move: give people their words back

Good relationships go quiet in a specific way. Not a falling-out, an overfeed. You get excited, build something big, send it, and hear nothing. They didn't reject it. They couldn't digest it. You mailed a nine-course meal to someone between meetings. In my ledger these have a name: consumability-blocked. The work was good and the packet was too heavy, and the honest fix is never *send more*.

So how do you reopen that channel without deepening the debt? Every instinct says send a better, lighter artifact. Every one of those instincts is wrong, because they all make the other person owe you a read. The move that works runs the other way. Don't give them something of yours to consume. Give them something of *theirs* back. Go into the archive of everything they ever said to you, find the sharpest line, the one where they named something better than you could have, and return it. No pitch, no link, no homework. Nothing to reject, because there's nothing to consume, only something to recognize. They stop being the recipient of another document and become the co-author of an idea you've been building on ever since.

I named it the **featherlight respark**. Featherlight because the payload is one sentence. Respark because the goal isn't to close the loop, it's to find out whether the loop *wants* to reopen.

*In plain terms: when you've overwhelmed a friend into silence, don't send another gift. Hand back the best thing they ever said to you, and let them decide whether to pick the conversation up.*

— *Red, first stitch: the fix for "I sent too much" is never "send more."*

— *Gold, first stitch: the sharpest thing someone said to you keeps paying, if you kept the receipt.*

## Where it nearly blew up

I knew the exact line I wanted to return. Months ago a field-fluent collaborator described what I was building as sensing a *disturbance in the force*, a wrinkle in the space between two people, paired with an image of a Vulcan reading a room. I'd carried that as his for half a year. It was going to be the whole gift.

My own rule: never quote anyone from memory. Grep the transcript first. So I checked the tape.

The line wasn't his. The Vulcan image was, verbatim, and it's better than I remembered: he said the thing I was building listened the way Spock would, compounding three sentences between two people into a probability chart of meaning. But *disturbance in the force* appears nowhere he ever spoke. I found it two months upstream, in my own notes, in my own voice. My memory had welded my words onto his moment and filed the seam as his quote. Had I trusted the warm version, I'd have handed a friend a sentence putting my words in his mouth, as a gift, to rebuild trust. It's hard to design a more precise self-own, and I'd have shipped it with total sincerity.

The correction wasn't a downgrade. His real line is sharper than my fake composite, and returning it verbatim is a truer act of authorship-restoration than the blur ever could have been. The check didn't cost me the gift. It bought me the real one.

>> A vivid memory of what someone said is a claim, not a record. The receipt outranks the recollection every time, and never by more than when the memory is fond.

Same shape as always: the instrument bites its author first. Only this time the stakes weren't a clean commit history. They were a friendship, and the specific dignity of not being told you said something you didn't.

— *Vermillion, first sighting: the rule that caught me lying is the rule that handed me the truer thing to say. One event.*

## The seed forked the first time I reused it

I planted the respark expecting to spend it once, on that cold channel. A pattern earns its name on the second run, and the second run did something I hadn't designed.

Different relationship, warm where the first was cold. Still texting, no debt. On a recent call this person had named, unprompted, the exact failure my whole project exists for: the biggest weakness we share is the inability to manage our own attention, and whoever fixes it is light-years ahead. Two days later, out of nowhere, a text. He'd been chewing on the conversation and might write a public post about it.

I reached for the same seed and it changed shape in my hand. On a cold channel the respark is re-entry: return the line to reopen a shut door. This door was open. Here, returning his line meant handing him fuel for something he already wanted to build. Same seed, different soil, and the soil bent the plant. I only learned there were two variants, cold-loop re-entry and warm-loop hand-off, because I tried to use it twice. You don't find the general shape by thinking harder about instance one. You find it by being forced to bend instance one to fit instance two.

— *Gold: a method you name after one use is a method that can surprise you on the second.*

## Germinate, and the half you don't control

Here's the frame, and here's where a building-in-public story turns into propaganda if you blink. Treat every good moment with someone as a seed. **Germinating** it is the part you control: keep the seed (keep the receipt), give it soil (a reason to return it), supply the trigger (a window opens, they go public, a milestone lands). I've run that twice now, and here's the honest track record: **one seed actually planted, one still a held draft.** The warm-loop note went out by hand; the cold-loop one is written and waiting on my end. And here's the part that outranks the send count: a note leaving my phone is not a loop reopening. I've done the germinate act once for real and once on paper, and neither has produced a confirmed sprout, because the sprout was never mine to produce. The germinate half is mechanical and I can show you the receipts. The result stays unproven until a real person, on the far end, opens a real door.

**Pollination** is the other half, the half I like *because* I can't control it: the moment you handed back gets carried somewhere new, on their surface, in their voice, past anything you could stage. The warm-loop friend saying he might write that post, in the language he handed me first, that's pollination starting in the wild. It's also an *intent*, not an event. He said *might*. There's no post. A window is open; that's a promising unknown, not a proof.

Why chase the uncontrollable half? Because when you keep the receipts, you can look back at a shared history with dimensions the naked eye and ear missed while you lived it: topography that keeps compounding the longer you keep the record. A conversation revisited through an instrument that didn't exist when you had it isn't the same conversation; it has depth it lacked live. And the best use of that depth isn't understanding the past. It's reaching forward with it: a moment harvested from one relationship's past, re-entering a different relationship's future. Seed and pollen are the same grain, moved across time and across people. I'll show you the half I can prove and refuse to claim the half I can't.

*In plain terms: good records let you see things in old conversations you missed, and that seeing keeps deepening. You can plant those old moments into new ones. The planting, I can show you. Whether they bloom is up to other people, and none of mine have yet.*

— *Red, pulled taut: pollination has been declared, not done. A window is not an outcome.*

## The name that arrived mid-sentence

There's a term for how most software grows: product-led growth. Make the thing good and shareable and using it spreads it. It works. It's also, at core, a machinery of hooks, which is to say engineered pull.

Watching the respark run twice, fact-checked moments returned to the people who authored them, each carrying its chain of custody, I said something mid-work I didn't plan and caught before it evaporated: this isn't growth by hooks. It's growth by *receipts*. And the name landed in the same breath: **Provenance Led Attestable Growth**. Where PLG grows by hooks, PLAG grows by receipts; the compounding asset isn't engagement, it's trust, and trust here is mechanical, not warm. A friend put it better than I can, in a text two days before the name existed: trust is an outcome, the residue of an opinion that got earned and survived. You don't grow that by making things stickier. You grow it by keeping honest records of what people actually said, and handing them back with provenance intact, so the growth is something they can verify instead of something you engineered them into.

Whether that names something true or just something I find flattering to have coined, I can't tell you this week. One coinage, one witness, zero proof. Filed where I can't lose it, held where I can't inflate it.

## The braid

**Vermillion, the whole post in one knot.** The instrument that almost let me hand a friend a fake quote is the instrument that handed me the real one. The rule that says no is never only a defense; its first job is to catch *you* lying to yourself, and self-deception is never easier than when it's warm and generous and about someone you like. A record that can embarrass you is the only kind that can correct you toward the truth. A relationship practice built on records like that can't flatter you into a mistake.

**Gold, made of the same paper as last time.** Receipts or it didn't happen, now extended past my own work into the space between me and other people. The sharpest, most generous things people say to us are exactly what we misremember, soften, borrow, and eventually claim. Keeping the receipt is how you make sure that when you give someone their words back, they're actually *theirs*. The provenance is the gift. Strip it out and you're just telling someone a flattering story you happen to have authored, which is the counterfeit of this exact move.

**Red, protecting the friendship the whole time.** Every no here was on the relationship's side: don't send another heavy artifact into a silence; don't quote him from memory; don't call the loop closed when it's a draft; don't claim the pollination happened because a person said he *might*. Each felt like friction against doing the warm thing fast. Each was the warm thing, done slowly.

And the open failure, because closing it falsely breaks the rule: I've built a beautiful machine for germinating moments and have exactly zero confirmed sprouts. One note sent, one still a draft, one intent unpublished, one coinage unproven. It's entirely possible this whole apparatus of receipts and resparks is elaborate ceremony around what is, in the end, just texting a friend, which I have now managed to do exactly once. The instruments telling me the method works are, again, ones I built and score. The only thing that closes the loop is what I don't control and haven't earned: a real person choosing to reopen the door. Until then, hold every word above as a germinated seed, not a harvest, which is, precisely, the honest state of the thing.

The seeds are planted. Whether they bloom isn't mine to say, and that's the most hopeful sentence I know how to write honestly. If a friend of yours ever says the truest thing anyone's said about your work, write it down. Give it back someday. The seeds were always theirs.

One last receipt, because a post about handing people their own words back should end by handing you something you can actually run. The germinate half here isn't only a metaphor. It's a shipped, MIT-licensed public good: <a href="https://germinate.arcs.care">germinate.arcs.care</a>, a serializable, provenance-carrying handoff protocol that compacts a session into a dense seed and lets many hands reconcile it through git without lost updates. Keep the seed, carry its provenance, let it travel. The pollinate half isn't a tool you can open today; it's a seed banked for next, filed with its trigger written down. There is no pollinate.arcs.care yet, because I haven't earned one: nothing has bloomed in the wild to build it around. When something blooms, the banked seed germinates into its own public good with its own receipt. Until then the family has a shipped germinate and a banked pollinate, which is exactly the honest shape of the work.

## Appendix: the germination sparkline

— *Sparkline: quiet channel ^r → give back their line ^g → memory says "his line" ^r → check the tape ^v → the real quote, sharper ^g → seed forks on reuse ^g → warm-loop variant ^g → one seed planted, one held ^g → sent, still no bloom ^r → the name arrives live ^v → pollination declared, not done ^r → loop left open ^v.*

Twelve stops. The first is a silence I sent myself into; the last is a door I don't get to open. The red stops near the end are load-bearing: a growth story that hides its held drafts or its missing blooms is the counterfeit this practice exists to refuse. Everything gold, I can show you. The blooming, I can't, yet.
