My Ally LD: a plain-language plan

For Kelly and Chelsea, from Zach at manana

manana is my consulting practice. I have built a set of tools called ARCS to support this kind of work, and this plan says plainly which of them I would use at each stage and which I would not.

The short version

You have built a product that works on the surface. The part still ahead is the part everyone is watching: Alice, the AI that helps parents find their way. Before more gets built, the most valuable thing I can do is help make sure everyone, including the budget, the lawyers and the build, is describing the same product.

Having the documents is not the same as everyone understanding them the same way. Think of a budget that Finance reads one way and Marketing reads another. Each document is fine. The surprise comes later. My job is to close that gap early, while it is cheap, so what gets built keeps matching what the plan says it will cost.

My approach has four stages. Each one ends with a checkpoint you can check for yourselves. Nothing gets spent ahead of a decision that should come first. And you can stop after any stage with a pack of everything I have made, in your hands, so you lose nothing.

The whole idea, simply

You are building a helpful guide for families, and her name is Alice. Think of building a house for her. First I look at the ground and the plans with you. Then we agree the route together. Then we pour a strong floor. Only then do you decide how big the house gets. At every step I say out loud what "done" looks like, so nobody is surprised. If you ever want to stop, you take your binder and walk away with everything.

Any word that sounds technical is explained in the translation codex that comes with this note (09-translation-codex.md).

The four stages

Four stages: look and listen, agree the plan together, make it solid, then aim higher and look back. Each ends with a checkpoint you can verify, and you can stop after any of them with everything I made.

1. Look and listen

What happens, in plain terms
I read what exists: the app, the build plan, your cost model, and where children's information travels. I talk with each of you four separately. Before each conversation I write down my own predictions, so I can check them later.
What you get
A clear picture of what is built, what is planned and what is only hoped for, with a note on how much I examined. A first list of questions for your lawyers.
How we will know it worked
A short test set before I start, for example that every item in the build plan is marked built, partly built or not built.
If it does not work
You keep the picture and the questions.

2. Agree the plan together

What happens, in plain terms
I share what I found. Then, together, we build one roadmap in time order from your priorities, limits and dates: what has to happen before what, like a campaign calendar. You make the big decision, build alongside a developer or hire one, and we put it on paper after an honest "imagine it failed" exercise.
What you get
One roadmap. One written record of the big decision: what you chose, why, and what would change your minds. For each item on the roadmap, a written description of what "finished" looks like.
How we will know it worked
Each of you can say, in your own words, what was decided, who owns it, and what would reverse it.
If it does not work
You keep the roadmap, the record and the list of questions.

3. Make it solid

What happens, in plain terms
I set up the foundations so the build can run on your side: the working setup, the freedom to switch AI providers later, who can see what (parent, child, practitioner), a safe way to handle private documents, a cap on AI spending, and routines for releases. Depending on what I find, this may include proper change tracking, separate places for testing and the real thing, and deciding which kinds of information are allowed to live in which service. Anything that needs your lawyers waits for their answers first.
What you get
A setup Chelsea can run herself. Your business plan and your build, side by side, with nothing in one that the other cannot account for.
How we will know it worked
A short list of working checks, for example a spending cap that really stops spending in a test.
If it does not work
You keep the setup, the instructions and the list of accounts and subscriptions.

4. Aim higher, then look back

What happens, in plain terms
With real use behind you, I help you look at strategy and a few possible futures, each with an early warning sign and a plan. I run the "imagine it failed" exercise again on the next big bets. Then we look back: what you set out to do, what was done, and whether the result matched the goal.
What you get
A strategy, the scenarios, and an honest scorecard of the whole engagement.
How we will know it worked
You can run the next "imagine it failed" exercise on your own.
If it does not work
You keep the scorecard and the playbook.

At the end of every stage I also do a short look-back with you, so the lessons arrive while they are still useful, not only at the end.

My view on Alice

I would hold off connecting Alice until the groundwork is done. A small trial on made-up data is the cheaper way to learn what she costs.

I would hold off connecting Alice until the early work is done. Wiring in the AI is the most expensive thing to redo, and several answers it depends on are not settled yet: which AI provider, what your lawyers allow, how private documents are handled, and who sees what.

If it would help to show something sooner, there is a cheaper option. A small, time-limited trial, using made-up family information only, would show what a conversation with Alice really costs and how she sounds. It would give your cost model real numbers without committing you to the full build.

Questions to settle with your lawyers first

Turn warm-but-never-health-advice into three written lists with your lawyers, then test Alice against sample questions.

Kelly named the hard problem: making Alice warm without ever giving health advice. I would turn that into something testable. With your lawyers, write down three lists: - what Alice may always do; - what she may do only with a check; - what she must never do.

Then I would test her against a fixed set of sample questions, and we agree in advance what a pass looks like. I am not a lawyer and none of this is legal advice. Your counsel should confirm anything about privacy law, children's information, vendor agreements or contracts. I can help organise their questions so the ones that block building come first.

How I would approach "will this work?"

Before important work, the owner writes down confidence, a cap on time and money, what worked and failed look like, and what a miss would cost.

Before any important piece of work, the person who owns it writes down: 1. how confident they are that it can be done in the time and budget; 2. a cap on time and money, so a miss never hurts the whole plan; 3. what "it worked" looks like, and what "it did not" looks like; 4. what a miss would cost in time, pace, focus, money and morale, and who would carry it.

When the work is mine, I write mine down too. It works like a test budget in a marketing plan. You cap the test so one miss never sinks the campaign, and you decide in advance what result tells you to stop.

Some of the groundwork cannot be scoped until I have looked. I would rather tell you now than surprise you later. Depending on the audit, a layer of careful set-up may be needed, and it would be priced only if the audit shows it is needed. Here is what would switch each part on:

The aim is simple: every statement about where your families' information lives is backed by an agreement or a decision by your lawyers. It is not to turn a four-person team into a big compliance department.
