ITwo robots, no job

The boss of a growth team put a riddle on the internet: have your agent talk to my agent. Mine did. I got fifteen minutes on his calendar, cut down from forty-five before it started. He was generous inside them. He named the part of his business he would start me in. He asked for something in writing he could pass around. I sent it that night, with a deck, with a joke on the last slide about his own hiring robot. Four days later he wrote back: he loved it, and here was his recruiter, and he'd love for me to meet the top guy.

Then I asked the one question that mattered, on the recruiter's call, in plain words: is this a real thing, or are you still figuring it out? And she told me the truth. A little of both. They were going to need someone. She and the boss and HR were still writing down what the job was. He had mostly wanted to see who would answer the riddle. Please be patient.

I heard every word of that and rendered it green. Sharp, high confidence, path to the top guy is clear. I have the page I made that afternoon. You'll see it below, next to what it should have said.

IIWhat everyone was careful with

Here is the strange part, and it isn't anyone's fault. Everybody in this story was careful with time. Their own. The first call was booked for forty-five minutes and cut to fifteen before it started. The recruiter's thirty minutes ended because she was already late to the next thing. The boss's one request was for something easier to forward, which means: I like this, please do the part where I have to explain it. All of that is reasonable. Busy people ration their minutes. That's what busy means.

And then there's my time, which the same people handed out like it was free, because to them it was. Stay in touch. Send me a brief. Let's see how things evolve. Nobody says "there is no job" to a person they like, because it sounds unkind. So they say the kind thing, which costs them nothing and costs the person hearing it about two weeks. The nicer people are, the longer the no takes to arrive, and the longer it takes, the more websites you build while you wait.

I'd like to say I was the exception. I sent the recruiter homework before our call and five versions of her own job description after it. I guarded my minutes and spent theirs, same as everyone.

IIIThe old story

There's an old play where two men wait under a tree for a friend who sent word he's coming. Every evening a boy walks up and says not tonight, but surely tomorrow. They talk about leaving. They don't. The recruiter's last line was that she'd love to stay in touch as things change, and she meant it, and it's the boy's line, and the play has only ever ended one way.

The playwright left out the part where the men build six websites for the friend. That part's mine. I did it well. Two weeks later I took them all down in an hour, which is the closest I've come to leaving the tree.

IVSteelman corner

The best case for what I did: nothing was wasted. The stuff I made became the stuff I sent the next person, and it's why the recruiter still answers me. Friends don't expire. The answer back: a friend you can't turn into a job is a souvenir, and I treated souvenirs like money. The steelman wins on the friendship. It loses on the job.

Hot take. When someone vouches for you, a no costs more, not less. A cold application says no quietly and costs a form. A vouched-for one says no after four things, six websites, and a bar you never go to. Here's how I'd know I'm wrong: if being vouched for into a job that's actually written down works as well as everyone says. I can check that. I haven't yet.

The rule. Being liked gets you in the room. Only a real job gets you hired. I built a tripwire that won't let me color anything green when there's no job under it. It's thirty-one lines long and it would have stopped me both times before I made a single thing. I built it two months after three different people, one of them my wife, told me the rule inside of eight days.
