A skill that turns a client's ask into a reply, an agreement and a scope exhibit, all rendered from one record. It refuses to put out a number without a source, a quote where a range belongs, a sharing ban, or a per-task price list before the client has paid for the first step.

How to read this: the cold read below gives the reasons, then what it does, then the rules with their status today, then what is not proven yet. Everything else is folded under "Background".

Why

A small consulting proposal usually goes out as three or four hand-typed documents. They drift apart the first time a date or a price changes, and they tend to oversell.

What

One record per engagement. Every document renders from it, and checks run before anything is sent. It is the sibling of the honest resume skill, which refuses to claim what it cannot defend about a person.

Why now

Nothing outside forces it. The one reason is cost: the two client engagements done by hand this month are fresh, and the renderer already existed, so the line between "generic" and "this client" was cheap to draw now.

What it does

In
The client's ask, in their words: an email thread or call notes. Formal RFPs come later.
Record
One JSON file. Every value has a source (an email, a call, a decision, or "judgment") and a history that only grows.
Out
A reply email, a plain-language agreement, an Exhibit A listing what is delivered, and an internal page that goes from a one-line summary down to every value and its source.
Before send
Four checks on the text, then a send gate: a lawyer has read the agreement template, a pre-send review from the client's five seats is clean, and a written prediction of the client's reply, with odds and their basis, is on file.
Never
It does not send, publish or set your prices. You send.

The rules, and where each stands today

Ten rules. Eight came from the brief. I changed rule 2 so the paid first step stays a fixed fee, because it is the price of finding out and a range on a short step reads as evasive. Rules 9 and 10 are new. All of them now run in v0.1.

  1. 1Every number cites a source or says it is judgmentbuiltThe record refuses a value with no source; a check fails any figure typed into a template. Gap: numbers written as words get through.
  2. 2The first step is a fixed fee; later steps are ranges with written assumptionsbuilt, changedThe record refuses a later step that isn't a range linked to an assumption, each with the test that would settle it.
  3. 3A reliance disclaimer instead of a sharing banbuiltThe agreement must carry a reliance term; a check fails "may not be shared" and similar.
  4. 4No per-task price list before the first step is paidbuiltUntil the first step is paid, a check fails any figure above the engagement's ceiling, or three or more table rows with prices or hours.
  5. 5"Opinion, not legal advice" and "as is", and a lawyer reads the agreement oncebuiltA check needs both phrases. The send gate needs a lawyer-review date for the template version.
  6. 6Predict the reply before sending, score it afterpartlyThe prediction and its score live in the record and the send gate needs the prediction. Writing it to the decision ledger is still manual.
  7. 7A pre-send review from the client's seatspartlyThe send gate needs a clean review on file. The five seats (founder, finance, counsel, skeptic, busy reader) are a written checklist, not yet a preset in the review workflow.
  8. 8What is not proven yet is said out loudbuiltThe record refuses an empty list; the internal page shows it.
  9. 9A change after sending is shown, never silentbuilt, newOnce a send date is set, a changed value prints "changed on DATE, was X" in every document.
  10. 10Plain language, "I" to "you", no em-dashesbuilt, newA check fails "we", "our", "us" and em-dashes in client text.

One I left out on purpose: "price last". A written proposal reader often scrolls to the price first, and hiding it reads as a sales move.

Not proven yet

  • That honest proposals win more often than ordinary ones. There is no outcome data; rule 6 starts collecting it.
  • That the record fits a second kind of engagement. Only one client engagement has used the code; the other was built by hand.
  • That the agreement template holds up outside the one state it was drafted for.
  • That the fictional example is far enough from a real client that nobody can tell who inspired it.

Background

Who it is for

First: a solo consultant writing a small fixed-fee proposal to a private company that asked by email or on a call, with a paid first step and later steps not yet scoped. That is the only type v0.1 supports. Not for anyone who wants a persuasive pitch: it makes a proposal less impressive and more defensible on purpose.

What stays private

Your terms, rates, payment rules, state of law and voice live in a private version of the skill, kept apart from the public method the same way the resume skill is split. The public method ships one example whose names, figures, dates and step count are all invented, because the shape of a real engagement can identify a client on its own.

Left out of v0.1, each a later item: client page tabs and progress, RFP intake, PDFs and hosting, automatic ledger writes, any second kind of engagement.

Where it lives, and why not inside rfp-engine

rfp-engine reads a solicitation and answers with the exact clause, and its code refuses requests to draft proposal language or decide whether to bid. That refusal is why it can be trusted. A drafting skill in the same repo would break that rule or sit beside it as an exception. So they stay neighbours: one reads the RFP and returns cited asks, the other takes cited asks and writes an offer. A plugin is premature with one skill and one kind of engagement.

What a third engagement must prove before it generalizes
  1. A different shape: a formal RFP or a different kind of client.
  2. Run end to end by a session that did not build it, from the skill file alone.
  3. No hand edits to any rendered document.
  4. Renderer changes under about 50 lines and at most three new record keys. More means the record was fitted to the first client. (Thresholds are my judgment.)
  5. Each check's catches counted and published, zero included.
  6. The prediction scored after the client replies.
